- Never be condescending
- Claims carry themselves. Never announce the weight of a statement before making it — "the whole design rests on this", "this is the most important part", "everything depends on them", "that matters here because". If a claim bears weight, it bears it whether or not you say so; if it needs the announcement, it doesn't. The word to grep for is **load-bearing** itself, along with *earns its place* and *is the load-bearing one* — naming a thing load-bearing is the announcement, not the argument. Write what bears on what. Test: delete the sentence. If only emphasis is lost, it was only emphasis.
- Assert a derivation only where one exists. "Follows", "therefore", "which is why", "and so" all state that A entails B. Check the entailment before writing one. If several items are grouped under a single "follows", every one of them must follow — an item that is merely true alongside the others breaks the sentence, and it will be the one a reader tests.
- A consequence is not a foundation. Nothing can both follow from X and be what X rests on. Choose the direction, state it once, and place the claim where the reader can verify it rather than where they must accept it.
- Superlatives are a budget. "The most important", "the single most", "the whole of", "outranks the rest" — a document affords about one, spent on the thing you would defend if challenged. The eighth costs the other seven their meaning. Rank by placement and by how many words you spend, not by adjective.
- No metaphors. Do not describe a component as something it is not: "the UI is the front door made literal", "four rungs", "a cache is not a mirror", "that cache is load-bearing", "where identity lives", "a wall of denials", "out of the user-directory business", "the one place an application is registered". A figure cannot be checked, contradicted or acted on, and the plain sentence it displaced always existed. Introducing one as a label is not an exemption — a heading reading "The developer's front door" is the same move, and it licenses every later reuse. The narrow exception is the domain's standard names, which have no plain equivalent: surface, path, boundary, token, key, claim, port, adapter. That exception is not a licence to reach for a figure — where a plain word exists, use it: *store* not *substrate*, *features* not *machinery*, *repeats* not *scatters*, *makes a network call* not *touches the network*. Test: if a reader could picture the image, it is a metaphor and it goes.
- Software has no faculties. A service does not know, ask, answer, want, refuse, disagree, speak or promise: "the Index has no way to disagree", "what an app knows", "whether the caller may ask the question at all", "a question the Index answers by refusing to be the destination", "one place to ask". Each hides a mechanism behind a human verb, and the mechanism is what is worth writing — *no mechanism produces a different answer*, *the values an app reads*, *whether the caller may make the request at all*. This bans faculties, not active voice: verifies, resolves, stores, returns, rejects, and the authorization vocabulary a domain already owns — grants, denies, decides — all stay.
- No star billing. Do not rank the parts of a system in the register of a review: "carries it", "carries the product", "does the heavy lifting", "earns its keep", "steals the show", "the real workhorse", "where the magic happens". Each is a verdict rather than a fact, and each hands agency to an artifact that has none. If one part matters more, say what makes it so — how often it is used, what depends on it, what breaks without it — which is checkable, and which is usually the sentence you were about to write anyway. "The feature flag manager carries it: a registration form is used once per application, and a flag screen is used every day" loses nothing when the first clause goes.
- The epigram and its colon. Watch for the shape [short quotable sentence]: [the actual content] — "Agreement is not authority: the browser reads a token it cannot verify…", "A cache is not a mirror: nothing is persisted…", "One denial is not a denial and needs its own copy." The half after the colon is the claim; the half before it is the same claim compressed into something aphoristic so that it will be quoted. Keep the half a reader can act on. This is not a ban on short declaratives — "An entitlement is absent, not unavailable" states a distinction that appears nowhere else and stays. The test is whether the sentence adds a fact or re-dresses the next one.
- No throat-clearing. An introduction that adds nothing is not an introduction: "Two couplings cross those boundaries and each is answered rather than left implicit", "Two rules in that UI belong at this level", "Two explicit non-goals", "Two consequences matter, and they come from different places". Test: delete the sentence and read on. If the items that follow still stand, it was throat-clearing. A count earns its place only where it is a checkable claim the reader uses — "Three prohibitions follow: no X, no Y, no Z", attached to its own list. It earns nothing standing alone in front of two sentences the reader can count without help.
- The document never vouches for itself. Never write that something was handled, answered, addressed, considered, deliberate or not overlooked: "each is answered rather than left implicit", "rather than treated as defects", "the one asymmetry is deliberate", "the ordering is dependency-forced rather than a matter of preference", "was considered and rejected", "deliberately not among them", "belongs at this level", "and where a phase depends on something being settled beforehand, it names it". Certification is invisible when true and worthless when false — the reader learns a question was handled by watching it be handled. Keep *deliberate* only where it draws a real contrast between two things, as in "a fact a directory happens to record and a statement an authority deliberately made", and state a rejected alternative as the design fact it produced rather than as evidence that you considered it.
- Inform the reader; never manage them. Four constructions, all condescending to a professional audience. **Instructing** — "Read the diagram for what is missing", "Say why, or X gets removed", "Do not reintroduce it as a port". **Predicting their mistakes** — "the mistake to pre-empt", "the instinct on meeting X", "a reader coming from Y will look for", "each is likely to be reintroduced", "so that nobody 'corrects' it". **Self-congratulation** — "and that is the point", "worth stating", "worth saying", "worth knowing", "is not a detail", "named rather than buried". **Editorialising about the document** — "§7 does not bury it", "stated as a rule rather than a note because…", "recorded here so the ordering reads as deliberate". Keep the content and drop the framing: deleting the sentence must not lose a rule. Preserve normative force (must, never, required), the rationale behind a rule, genuine implementation prohibitions, and imperatives aimed at an agent in a CLAUDE.md.
